H-1B sponsorship for new grads: how it works now and how to find employers who sponsor

A plain guide for international students: OPT, STEM OPT, the H-1B lottery and its recent changes, which employers sponsor, and how to ask about sponsorship without hurting your chances.

If you're an international student in the US, your job search has an extra layer: you need an employer who will hire you on OPT and, eventually, sponsor a work visa. That narrows the field and adds deadlines most of your classmates don't have. This guide explains how the system works for new grads, what changed recently, how to find employers who actually sponsor, and how to talk about sponsorship in applications and interviews.

Important: immigration rules change, and this is general information, not legal advice. Before you make decisions, check the official USCIS pages and talk to your school's international student office (your DSO) or an immigration attorney.

The usual path: F-1, OPT, STEM OPT, then H-1B

  1. F-1 student status while you study.
  2. OPT (Optional Practical Training): up to 12 months of work authorization after graduation in a job related to your field. You apply to USCIS with your school's recommendation, and you can apply up to 90 days before your program end date.
  3. STEM OPT extension: if your degree is on the STEM designated list, you can extend OPT by 24 more months. Your employer must use E-Verify and sign a training plan (Form I-983).
  4. H-1B: the main long term work visa for professional jobs. Your employer files for you, and for most private employers that means entering the annual lottery.

The key point: OPT plus STEM OPT gives STEM grads up to three years of work authorization, which means up to three chances at the H-1B lottery. Non STEM grads get one year of OPT and, usually, one chance. That difference shapes which employers and roles make sense for you.

The unemployment clock

On OPT you can be unemployed for up to 90 days in total. A STEM OPT extension adds 60 more days, for 150 days across the whole period. Time between graduation and your first job counts once your OPT start date arrives. That's why it matters to start your search early, ideally two or three months before you graduate, and to track your days carefully.

How the H-1B lottery works

Most private employers are subject to an annual cap of 85,000 new H-1B visas: 65,000 regular and 20,000 more for people with a US master's degree or higher. Because far more people register than there are visas, USCIS runs a selection each spring:

  • Employers register candidates electronically, usually in March.
  • USCIS selects registrations, and selected employers file full petitions.
  • Approved H-1B employment usually starts on October 1.

The rules around selection have changed recently. DHS moved to a selection process weighted toward higher wage levels, and in 2025 a presidential proclamation added a large fee for certain new H-1B petitions. Guidance has said that fee doesn't apply to people changing status inside the US, which covers most F-1 students moving from OPT to H-1B, but details have shifted and may shift again. Check the current USCIS H-1B page and ask your DSO before each cap season.

Cap exempt employers: the overlooked route

Some employers aren't subject to the lottery at all. Universities, nonprofits affiliated with universities, and nonprofit or government research organizations can file H-1B petitions any time of year. That includes many university hospitals, research institutes and national labs. If you're in data, research, healthcare or engineering, cap exempt employers are worth a serious look. You skip the lottery entirely.

How to find employers who sponsor

1. Use the public data

The USCIS H-1B Employer Data Hub lists employers who filed petitions, with approval counts by year. The Department of Labor publishes LCA (Labor Condition Application) data with job titles, locations and wages. Together they tell you which companies sponsor for your role and what they paid.

2. Look at who sponsors your role, not just who sponsors

Big tech, consulting firms and banks file the most petitions, but they sponsor mostly for engineering, data and finance roles. A company that sponsors hundreds of software engineers may never sponsor a marketing coordinator. Search the LCA data by job title.

3. Read the posting carefully

Many postings say outright "we are unable to sponsor" or "must be authorized to work without sponsorship now or in the future". Skip those. Others say nothing. Silence isn't a no, especially at companies with a sponsorship history.

4. Prioritize roles with strong sponsorship history

Software engineering, data science, data engineering, machine learning, quantitative finance and some product roles have the deepest sponsorship history. In our feed there are currently 1,441 entry level software engineer roles and 220 entry level data scientist roles in the US, so the pool is large even after filtering out companies that don't sponsor.

5. Be careful with defense and government work

A large share of entry level engineering hiring right now comes from defense and aerospace companies. Many of those roles require US citizenship or a security clearance, which rules out most international students. Check the posting before applying.

How to talk about sponsorship

  • On applications, answer the sponsorship question honestly. Lying creates problems later and can cost you the offer.
  • In your first recruiter call, confirm early and simply: "I'm on F-1 with STEM OPT, so I'm authorized to work for up to three years, and I'd need H-1B sponsorship after that. Is that something your team supports?" Recruiters appreciate clarity.
  • Lead with authorization, not the visa. Saying you can work for up to three years without any cost to them is a strong, true opening.
  • Know the employer's steps: E-Verify for STEM OPT, the I-983 training plan, and the lottery timeline. Employers new to sponsorship sometimes need a nudge, and you look prepared.

Backup plans that people use

  • Cap exempt employers (universities, affiliated nonprofits, research labs).
  • Graduate study that qualifies for a new period of OPT or the master's cap.
  • Working abroad for a US company for a year, which can open the L-1 transfer route at large employers.
  • O-1 visas for people with strong records of achievement, which some early career researchers and founders qualify for.

Each has tradeoffs and eligibility rules. Talk them through with your DSO or an attorney.

How to search sponsorship data, step by step

  1. Open the USCIS H-1B Employer Data Hub and search for a company you're interested in. You'll see approvals by fiscal year, which tells you whether they sponsor at all and how often.
  2. Open the Department of Labor's LCA disclosure data (or a site that indexes it) and search by job title, such as "Data Analyst" or "Product Manager". You'll see which employers filed for that title, the work location and the wage.
  3. Build a target list of 30 to 50 employers that have sponsored your title in the last two years, in locations you'd work.
  4. Check their current openings and apply to roles that match your years.

This list becomes the backbone of your search. It's far more efficient than applying broadly and hoping.

Roles with the deepest sponsorship history

Historically, the largest share of H-1B petitions has gone to computer related occupations, followed by engineering, finance, research and healthcare roles. In practical terms: software engineering, data science, data engineering, machine learning, quantitative and financial analysis, and research roles at universities and labs offer the most paths. Product and marketing roles sponsor too, but less often and mostly at larger companies.

Check whether your degree is STEM designated

STEM OPT eligibility depends on your degree's CIP code being on the STEM designated list, not on how technical your coursework felt. Some business analytics, economics and management programs are STEM designated; others aren't. Check your I-20 or ask your DSO early, because it can change your whole timeline.

Sponsorship questions in interviews, and good answers

  • "Will you require sponsorship?" "I'm authorized to work on STEM OPT for up to three years. I'd need H-1B sponsorship at some point in that window."
  • "What does that involve for us?" "For STEM OPT, enrolling in E-Verify if you aren't already, and a short training plan form. For H-1B, registering me in March, which your immigration counsel would handle."
  • "What if you're not selected?" "With STEM OPT I'd have up to three chances. I'm also open to other options your team uses."

Calm, factual answers make sponsorship feel like a manageable process instead of a risk.

Mistakes international students make

  • Starting the search too late, which eats into the unemployment clock.
  • Applying to roles that say "no sponsorship" and hoping for an exception.
  • Not tracking unemployment days precisely.
  • Ignoring cap exempt employers, which skip the lottery entirely.
  • Relying on informal advice instead of your DSO and official USCIS guidance.

Questions

How long can I work on OPT?

Up to 12 months after graduation. STEM graduates can extend by 24 months with an employer that uses E-Verify, for up to three years in total.

How many days can I be unemployed on OPT?

Up to 90 days on regular OPT, plus 60 more days with a STEM OPT extension, for 150 days in total.

What is the H-1B cap?

85,000 new visas a year for most private employers: 65,000 regular and 20,000 for holders of a US master's degree or higher, allocated through a selection each spring.

What does cap exempt mean?

Universities, affiliated nonprofits and nonprofit or government research organizations can sponsor H-1B visas any time of year without the lottery.

How do I find companies that sponsor H-1B?

Use the USCIS H-1B Employer Data Hub and Department of Labor LCA data, search by your job title, and check each posting's sponsorship language. Always confirm current rules with your school's DSO.
