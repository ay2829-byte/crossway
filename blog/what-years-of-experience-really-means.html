What "3+ years of experience" really means: we read the years line on 83,842 job postings

The most common years requirement is 3, a third of postings accept 0 to 2, and 46% of roles titled entry level still ask for 3 or more. How to read the years line and when to apply anyway.

"3+ years of experience required." It's the line that makes people close a tab. But how strict is it, how common is it, and when should you apply anyway? To answer that properly, we looked at the years line across Crossway's job feed: 180,000 live roles pulled from company career pages in November 2026, of which 83,842 state a minimum number of years.

How many years postings actually ask for

Minimum yearsPostingsShare
0 to 228,16734%
3 to 426,26131%
5 to 614,11717%
7 or more15,29718%

The single most common requirement is 3 years (19,098 postings), followed by 2 years (17,010), 5 years (11,413) and 1 year (11,157). Requirements cluster on round numbers: 5, 8, 10 and 12 are far more common than 6, 9 or 11. That tells you something important: these numbers are usually a rough signal of level, picked by habit, not a precise threshold someone measured.

It varies a lot by role

The share of postings that accept 0 to 2 years, among those that state years:

If you're early in your career, this table is worth more than any resume tip. Applying to product manager or growth roles with one year of experience means fighting the odds on nine applications out of ten. Applying to analyst roles, you're squarely in the target range almost half the time.

Titles and years often disagree

Here's the part that trips people up. Of the postings with an entry level or junior title that state years, 46% still ask for 3 or more. Meanwhile, 19% of postings with a plain, mid level title ask for 5 or more. The title alone is a poor guide. Read the years line, and when there isn't one, look at the responsibilities: "own the roadmap for a product area" and "mentor junior team members" mean senior, whatever the title says.

How strict is the years line?

Recruiters and hiring managers say the same thing in different words: the years line is a filter for the pile, not a law. In practice:

  • Within about a year is usually fine. If the posting says 3+ and you have 2 with strong, relevant work, apply. Lead with the evidence.
  • Two or more years short is a long shot, especially at large companies where recruiters screen with simple rules.
  • Ranges are more honest. "2 to 4 years" usually means they'll consider 2 and are budgeted for 4. "5+" with no upper bound often means "senior".
  • "Or equivalent experience" genuinely widens the door. Projects, research, freelance work and internships can count.
  • Regulated roles (finance licenses, healthcare, government) are strict. Treat those numbers literally.

What counts as a year

  • Full time work in the field counts fully.
  • Adjacent work often counts partly. An analyst applying for product roles can count the years they worked closely with product teams, if they explain it.
  • Internships usually count for entry level roles and rarely for "3+" roles.
  • Graduate school sometimes counts for research and data science roles, especially a thesis with real data.
  • Freelance and founding a company count if you can show results and scope.

A simple rule for when to apply

  1. You meet the years line, or you're within one year and have strong proof: apply.
  2. You're two years short but match the skills unusually well: apply with a referral, or don't apply.
  3. Three or more years short: skip it and spend the time on roles where you qualify.

The goal is to spend your application time where your odds are real. Ten applications to roles you fit beat fifty to roles that will auto reject you.

When the posting doesn't say

About half of postings in our feed don't state years at all. For those, use the level in the title (intern, entry, associate, senior, staff, principal) and the scope of the responsibilities. On Crossway, when a posting doesn't list years we estimate from the title and label it clearly as an estimate.

How to frame your years when you're short

If you're a year short, don't hide it. Reframe what you have:

  • Count relevant time precisely. "Two and a half years in analytics, including 18 months owning the pricing dashboard" is stronger than "2 years".
  • Show scope. Owning a project end to end, leading people or working with senior stakeholders signals more than the calendar does.
  • Show speed. Promotions, quickly expanded responsibilities or results in a short time all suggest you'll grow into the role.
  • Add proof. A case study or solved problem that shows the level of work they need.

Reading years in context: three examples

"Data Analyst, 2+ years of SQL experience"

With one year of daily SQL at work plus strong projects, apply. The requirement is about SQL fluency, which you can show in a screen.

"Product Manager, 5+ years of product management experience"

With two years as an analyst, skip it. The gap is large, and "product management experience" is specific.

"Software Engineer II, 2 to 4 years"

New grad with two internships and a strong project? It's a stretch but worth applying, especially with a referral. Internships at the same kind of company help.

A note for employers

If you're hiring, the data suggests years lines are often picked by habit. Requirements cluster on round numbers, and nearly half of entry level titled roles ask for 3+ years, which shuts out the people the title invites. Consider describing the skills and scope you need instead, or asking candidates to show the work. On Crossway, companies see proof of work first, and years are one signal among several.

Quick answers to common worries

  • "Will they think I didn't read the posting?" Not if your application makes the case clearly in the first lines.
  • "Does part time work count?" Partly. Describe it honestly by scope, not just dates.
  • "What about a career gap?" Gaps are common. A one line explanation is enough.

What the years line usually signals, level by level

Years askedWhat it usually meansWhat they expect you to do
0 to 1New grad or first jobLearn fast, execute well defined tasks, ask good questions
2 to 3Early career, independent on most tasksOwn a project or workstream with light guidance
4 to 6Mid to seniorOwn an area, make calls in ambiguity, guide others
7 to 9Senior to staffSet direction across teams, mentor, handle the hardest problems
10+Principal, director or aboveShape strategy and lead leaders

Read your own experience against the right column, not just the left. Someone with two years who already owns a workstream end to end is closer to a "3+" role than the calendar suggests. Someone with five years of narrow, task level work may not be ready for a role that expects them to own an area.

How the years line connects to pay

Companies usually set a pay band for a level, then write a years line to match it. That's why a "3+" role at one company and a "5+" role at another can pay the same: they're the same level, described differently. When you're deciding whether to stretch for a role, look at the posted range too. If the pay band is far above anything you've earned, the level is probably well above you, whatever the years line says. If it's close to what you earn now, a stretch application is more reasonable.

A 30 second checklist before you apply

  1. Is the years line within one year of my relevant experience?
  2. Do the responsibilities describe work I've done at least once?
  3. Is the pay band close to my level?
  4. Was it posted in the last two weeks?
  5. Can I show one piece of proof that matches the core of the role?

Four or five yeses: apply today. Two or fewer: move on, and spend the time on a better fit.

How we counted

We read the minimum years stated in each posting's text across Crossway's feed of 180,000 roles pulled from company career pages in November 2026. Phrases like "3+ years", "at least five years", "2 to 4 years" and "minimum of 3 years of relevant experience" count; phrases like "company founded 10 years ago" don't. Postings that list no number are left out of the shares.
