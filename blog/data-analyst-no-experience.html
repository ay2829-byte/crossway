How to get a data analyst job with no experience (with what employers are asking for right now)

44% of data analyst postings that state years accept 0 to 2. The skills they ask for, what entry level analysts earn, and a step by step plan to land your first role.

Data analyst is one of the most open entry points into a professional career. Unlike product management, where only about one posting in eleven accepts two years or less, data analyst roles are genuinely open to newcomers. This guide uses live data from Crossway's job feed to show what employers ask for, what entry level analysts earn, and the exact plan we'd follow to land a first analyst job with no prior experience.

How open data analyst jobs really are

In early November 2026, Crossway's feed held 234 data analyst roles in the US posted in the previous 45 days, with 95 posted in the last week alone. Of the 107 postings that state a minimum number of years:

Almost half accept 0 to 2 years, and by title or years, 117 of the 234 roles are entry level. Compare that with product manager roles, where 9% accept 0 to 2 years. If you want a data career, analyst is the door that's actually open.

What entry level data analysts earn

Among entry level analyst postings that list pay, the typical range is $80k to $110k. Across all analyst levels, it's $100k to $123k. Remote analyst roles in our feed list lower ranges, around $77k to $93k, because many are contract or support roles.

Data scientist roles pay more (typically $143k to $210k across levels) but are harder to enter: 27% of those postings accept 0 to 2 years. Many people start as analysts and move into data science after one or two years.

The skills employers actually ask for

Across data analyst postings, the most requested skills are, in order: SQL, Excel, Python, statistics, Power BI, Tableau, machine learning and Snowflake. Here's how to read that list:

  • SQL is non negotiable. It's the first skill on almost every posting and the first round of almost every interview. If you learn one thing, learn SQL well: joins, aggregation, window functions, dates and cleaning messy text.
  • Excel still matters, especially pivot tables, lookups and simple models. Many analyst teams live in spreadsheets.
  • Pick one BI tool. Power BI shows up slightly more often than Tableau in our feed, especially at larger companies. Either one is fine. Employers care that you can build a clear dashboard, not which tool you used.
  • Python is a strong plus. pandas for cleaning and analysis, plus basic charts. You don't need machine learning for an entry level analyst job.
  • Statistics: averages and medians, distributions, confidence intervals and A/B test basics. Enough to avoid wrong conclusions.

Who hires entry level analysts

Entry level analyst hiring in our feed is spread across banks (Capital One is the most active), defense and government contractors (RTX, Anduril, General Dynamics IT, Guidehouse), fintech (Sezzle) and retailers (The Home Depot). A few patterns:

  • Banks and insurers hire analysts in volume and train them well. Titles include data analyst, business analyst and reporting analyst.
  • Government contractors hire steadily, but many roles need US citizenship or a clearance. Check before applying.
  • Healthcare is a large, underrated employer of analysts, with titles like healthcare data analyst or quality analyst.
  • Tech companies hire fewer pure entry level analysts and often want SQL plus product sense.

Watch out for titles that use "analyst" for very different jobs. Benefits analyst, credit analyst, threat analyst and financial analyst roles are not data analyst roles, even though job boards often lump them together.

A step by step plan for your first analyst job

Step 1: Learn SQL to interview level (3 to 4 weeks)

Aim to solve medium problems on joins, grouping and window functions in under 15 minutes. Practice on realistic tables with customers, orders and events, not textbook examples. Our 25 SQL interview questions for data analysts covers the patterns that come up again and again.

Step 2: Build two portfolio projects (3 to 4 weeks)

Skip the Titanic dataset. Pick public data close to the industry you want: city 311 requests, public company filings, a public health dataset, or your own spending data. Each project should answer one clear business question, show the SQL or Python, include one clean dashboard, and end with a recommendation in plain words.

Step 3: Write it up like a work memo

Hiring managers want to see that you can explain a result to someone who doesn't care about your code. One page: the question, what you found, what you'd do, and what you're unsure about.

Step 4: Apply where you qualify

Filter for roles that accept 0 to 2 years and entry level titles. Apply to around ten a week with a short, specific note. Don't waste time on 5+ year roles.

Step 5: Prepare for the interview loop

Expect a SQL screen (live or take home), an Excel or case question, a metrics question ("how would you measure this?") and behavioral questions. Practice explaining your projects in two minutes.

If you're changing careers

Career changers often have an advantage they don't use: domain knowledge. A former nurse who learns SQL is a strong candidate for healthcare analytics. A former teacher is a natural fit for education data teams. A former retail manager understands inventory and sales data better than most new grads. Lead with that, and point your portfolio projects at that industry.

What your first year looks like

Most first year analysts spend their time cleaning data, building and maintaining reports, and answering ad hoc questions from other teams. The analysts who grow fastest do three things: they ask why a question matters before answering it, they automate repetitive reports, and they bring a recommendation, not just a chart. After a year or two, common next steps are senior analyst, analytics engineer, product analyst or data scientist.

Five portfolio project ideas that impress hiring managers

  1. City service requests: use a city's open 311 data to find which neighborhoods wait longest for fixes, and why. Show the SQL, one map or chart, and a recommendation for the city.
  2. Public company metrics: pull quarterly numbers for three competitors from their filings, build a simple comparison dashboard, and write what it says about their strategies.
  3. An A/B test write up: use a public experiment dataset and walk through the test design, the result, the confidence interval and the decision you'd make.
  4. Messy data cleanup: take a deliberately messy export (or make one), clean it in SQL, and document every decision. Hiring managers value this because it's most of the real job.
  5. Your own data: spending, workouts or listening history, analyzed with a clear question and an honest conclusion.

Each project should fit on one page plus an appendix. The page matters more than the code.

Questions you'll likely get, and how to answer

"Walk me through a project."

Use a simple arc: the question, the data and what was wrong with it, what you did, what you found, and what you'd recommend. Two minutes, no jargon.

"How would you measure the success of a new feature?"

Name the goal, a main metric, two supporting metrics and one guardrail. Our metrics interview guide has worked examples.

"Here's a table. Find the top 3 customers by revenue in each region."

This is the classic window function question. Practice it until you can write it from memory; it's in our SQL interview questions.

"Tell me about a time your analysis was wrong."

Pick a real example, explain how you found the mistake, and what you changed in your process. Interviewers want to hear that you check your work.

Do certifications help?

Certificates can help you learn in a structured way, and some employers list tools like Power BI or Tableau. But in our experience, hiring managers weigh a strong portfolio and a good SQL screen far more than a certificate. If you take one, finish it with a project you can show.

Titles to search besides "data analyst"

  • Business intelligence analyst or BI analyst
  • Reporting analyst
  • Insights analyst
  • Analytics analyst or analytics associate
  • Operations analyst on a data heavy team
  • Product analyst, usually a bit more technical

Questions

Can you get a data analyst job with no experience?

Yes. In our November 2026 count, 44% of data analyst postings that state years accepted 0 to 2, and half of all US analyst roles were entry level by title or years.

What skills do I need for an entry level data analyst job?

SQL first, then Excel, one BI tool like Power BI or Tableau, basic statistics, and ideally Python with pandas.

How much does an entry level data analyst make?

Entry level analyst postings that list pay typically range from $80k to $110k a year in the US.

How long does it take to become a data analyst?

With focused practice, about 2 to 4 months to reach interview level in SQL and build two solid portfolio projects.

Do I need a degree to be a data analyst?

Many postings list a degree, but plenty of employers hire on demonstrated skills. Strong SQL and a clear portfolio carry a lot of weight.
